## Statements

A statement takes the next clause number, in the paragraphs' series, so <Ref to="mean-value" /> is cited by the number in its margin.

<Statement kind="definition" id="differentiable" name="Differentiable on an interval">

A function $f$ is **differentiable on** $(a, b)$ when $f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$ exists at every $x \in (a, b)$.

</Statement>

<Statement kind="theorem" id="mean-value" name="Mean value">

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. Then for some $c \in (a, b)$,

$$
f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.
$$

In particular, if $f(a) = f(b)$, then $f'(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (a, b)$.

</Statement>

<Proof of="mean-value">

The function

$$
g(x) = f(x) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a)
$$

is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, with $g(a) = g(b) = f(a)$. So $g$ has an extremum at some $c \in (a, b)$, where $g'(c) = 0$, which is $f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.

</Proof>

<Ref to="mean-value" /> asks for <Ref to="differentiable" /> only inside the interval, not at its
ends.

### Consequences

<Statement kind="lemma" id="zero-derivative">

- If $f'(x) = 0$ for every $x \in (a, b)$, then $f$ is constant on $[a, b]$.

</Statement>

<Statement kind="corollary" id="same-derivative" name="Functions with the same derivative differ by a constant">

If $f' = g'$ on $(a, b)$, then $f - g$ is constant on $[a, b]$.

</Statement>

Applying <Ref to="zero-derivative" /> to $f - g$ gives <Ref to="same-derivative" />.

A proof that doesn't follow its statement names it:

<Proof of="zero-derivative">

For $x \in (a, b]$, <Ref to="mean-value" /> on $[a, x]$ gives some $c \in (a, x)$ with

$$
f(x) - f(a) = f'(c)\,(x - a) = 0.
$$

</Proof>

A proof right after another statement still names the one it proves, and stands clear of it. This one opens with an equation and ends with a list, whose last item carries the square:

<Statement kind="theorem" id="rolle" name="Rolle">

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$ and $f(a) = f(b)$, then $f'(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (a, b)$.

</Statement>

<Statement kind="corollary" id="one-crossing">

If $f'$ has no zero in $(a, b)$, then $f$ takes each value at most once on $[a, b]$.

</Statement>

<Proof of="rolle">

$$
\max_{[a, b]} f \quad \text{and} \quad \min_{[a, b]} f
$$

both exist, since $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.

- If both equal $f(a)$, then $f$ is constant, and any $c \in (a, b)$ will do.
- Otherwise one of them is reached at some $c \in (a, b)$, an interior extremum, where $f'(c) = 0$.

</Proof>

<Proof of="one-crossing">

- If $f(x) = f(y)$ with $a \le x < y \le b$, then <Ref to="rolle" /> on $[x, y]$ gives a zero of $f'$ in $(x, y)$.
- So no two points of $[a, b]$ share a value.

</Proof>

### Examples

A worked example is a statement too. Its solution follows a run-in label, shown openly, and its equations, tables and figures keep the page's numbering:

<Statement kind="example" id="parabola-tangent" name="A parabola’s parallel tangent">

Where in $(0, 2)$ is the tangent to $f(x) = x^2$ parallel to the secant from $0$ to $2$?

---

The secant's slope is

$$
\frac{f(2) - f(0)}{2 - 0} = \frac{4 - 0}{2} = 2,
$$

and $f'(c) = 2c$, so the $c$ of <Ref to="mean-value" /> is $1$:

| $x$ | $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ |
| --: | -----: | ------: |
|   0 |      0 |       0 |
|   1 |      1 |       2 |
|   2 |      4 |       4 |

The tangent at $1$ has the secant's slope, $2$.

</Statement>
